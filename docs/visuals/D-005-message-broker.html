Decision brief · D-005, D-027, D-028 · 2026-11-22 · decided: no message broker, after trying one

A message broker: when it becomes the right choice, and how it would slot in

The first version delivers changes with a log in PostgreSQL and one worker per receiver. A broker becomes right when receivers multiply, belong to other teams, or need to work in parallel. Because the rules that matter stay the same, adding one later replaces a single layer.

When you would need one

Any one of these is a reason; none is true of this product today.

How you would notice in practice: receivers falling behind while their own systems are healthy; the database busy serving the workers' reads; a second team asking for "the events". Until then a broker is one more system that has to be up, and it would replace delivery machinery that is already built small and tested.

What trying it showed (E-006)

You chose a broker (D-027) so the system could ship alongside other teams' services on standard tooling. Before rewriting the design around it, Apache Kafka was put in the middle of a small build and broken on purpose. The promises held, with two things built on top, and it cost speed and the independence of organizations. You then decided to go back (D-028).

Kafka ran as one node with near-default settings; a tuned setup would be faster. The delay between organizations is structural: Kafka keeps order per lane, and several organizations share a lane.

Why adding it later is cheap

Three things carry the guarantees, and none of them is the broker: the change and its log entry saved in one step; receivers that ignore repeats and older versions; the feed worked out from the stream in order. With a broker those stay exactly as they are. Only the layer between the log and the receivers changes: one relay publishes the log into the broker, and each receiver reads the broker from its own place. The picture below is that later shape.

1 · The later shape, with a broker

Replaced by the broker
  • Our own delivery workers and their table of places.
  • Our own "parking place": a standard side stream for changes a receiver refuses.
  • Our own way of telling how far behind a receiver is.
  • The separate catch-up paths for the live connection and for agents: they become ordinary readers.
Stays exactly as designed
  • Saving the change and its log entry in one step, in PostgreSQL.
  • Receivers that ignore repeats and older versions; a delete wins.
  • The feed worked out from the stream, fenced when it chokes, rebuilt by replay.
  • The feed deciding notifications; the hold measured by the stream's own clock.

2 · What a broker would give

ReasonWhat it means here
It is the machinery we were writing by hand.An ordered log that is kept, a remembered place per reader, replay from the start: that is our design, and it is also the definition of a log-style broker. Our first experiment found three faults in my hand-written version of it. The broker's version has been run by thousands of teams.
Adding a receiver touches nothing else.A fourth or fifth system (analytics, an audit trail, a second search engine) is a new reader with its own place. Conversations and the other receivers do not change and do not know.
One slow receiver cannot hurt the others or the database.A receiver that is down for an hour falls behind in the broker, alone. The database people type into is not the thing every receiver keeps asking.
The road to a larger size is configuration.Today one stream in one lane. Later the stream is split into lanes by channel, with several copies of a receiver working side by side, order kept inside each channel. In the draft this was the item "split the log by conversation"; with a broker it is a setting plus the choice of key.
Screens and agents become ordinary readers.Several live-connection servers can each read the stream and push to their screens. That is the step the draft listed under "at a hundred times this size".
It is what the products we studied run.Every product in our research saves first, publishes each change, and lets search, notifications and the feed consume it from a queue; Slack's own write-up puts Kafka at the front of that path (R-002). A reviewer recognises the shape without explanation.

3 · What a broker would not do

These are why the first version loses nothing by waiting: the hard parts have to be built either way, and they are.

not solved by a broker Getting changes into it safely

Writing to the database and publishing to the broker are two steps; a crash between them loses a change or announces one that was never saved. We keep "save and log in one step" and a relay that publishes the log. The broker does not replace this; it sits after it.

not solved by a broker Repeats

The relay may publish a change twice, and a reader may read one twice after a restart. A broker's "exactly once" holds only inside the broker, not for a search index or a push service outside it. Receivers still ignore repeats and older versions.

not solved by a broker Order across lanes

Order is kept inside one lane only. The feed needs "was it read before it was deleted", so a conversation's messages and its readers' actions must travel in the same lane. We start with one lane (total order) and name the key for splitting: the channel.

4 · Questions and answers, with or without a broker

QuestionAnswer
Why is there no message broker?Because nothing it is good at is needed yet (see the table at the top), and everything that makes delivery reliable has to be built with or without one. We built those parts, tested them by breaking them, and kept the door open: adding a broker replaces one layer. A broker at 1,500 messages a day would be one more system to keep running for no gain we could show.
Why not publish to the broker straight from the request?Two writes that cannot be made together. Shown by test: with the log entry saved in a second step and the process dying in between, 4 of 635 messages lost a change (E-003). So the change and its log entry are one database transaction, and a relay publishes afterwards.
The broker offers exactly-once. Why do receivers still check versions?That guarantee covers reading from and writing to the broker itself. A search index or a push provider is outside it. At the boundary the honest promise is "at least once, and a repeat changes nothing". Shown by test: without the version rule, 17 of 664 messages ended wrong under repeats.
What happens when the broker is down?People keep working: changes are saved and wait in PostgreSQL. Search, the feed and notifications fall behind and say so. When the broker returns, the relay continues from where it stopped. The broker being down never loses or refuses a message.
What happens when a receiver is down, for longer than the broker keeps data?PostgreSQL stays the truth, and the stream can be published again from the log. At this size the stream is simply kept in full.
How is order guaranteed?The log in PostgreSQL has one order (numbers handed out one writer at a time; without that rule a reader missed about a fifth of the changes, E-004). The relay publishes in that order into one lane. Every change carries its number, and the feed takes only the next one.
And when you split into lanes?By channel, so a channel's messages, threads and its readers' reads and dial stay in order. What crosses lanes (a person's Focus rules, for example) takes effect "from about then" instead of at an exact position; a rebuild may place a row that arrived in that same instant differently. We would say so, not hide it.
A change one receiver cannot process?Search: after three tries it goes to a side stream, visible, and can be sent again. The feed: the change is set aside and only the conversation or reader it touches is fenced; the rest stays exact (E-003: 51 rows fenced, 452 exact).
Why not read the database's own replication log instead of a relay?That is the next step at larger size and removes the one-writer-at-a-time rule. It also adds another moving part. For a first version a relay over a log table is fewer things to run and was tested.
How do you know any of this works?By breaking it. Every promise has a test that injects the failure: processes killed mid-burst, receivers stopped, slowed and wiped, half of all deliveries repeated or late. E-003 and E-004 are the record.

5 · If the day comes: which broker, and what to prove again

Apache Kafka would be the first candidate: it is the standard, and its model (a log that is kept, a remembered place per reader, replay from the start) is our design word for word. NATS JetStream is lighter and less widely known. RabbitMQ in its classic form hands a message over and forgets it, which does not fit rebuilding a receiver by replay. Redis Streams keeps data in memory first and promises less about not losing it.

What would have to be tested again with the broker in the middle: the relay killed between publishing and noting how far it got; the broker stopped mid-burst while people keep working; a receiver stopped, and wiped and rebuilt from the start of the stream; freshness against the 2-second target.

How this was decided, all on 2026-11-22: you first chose a broker, read the case for it and decided against (D-005); chose one again for standard tooling alongside other teams' services (D-027); and after it was tried, went back to the design without one, keeping it ready for many organizations (D-028). A middle way stays open for later: our own receivers fed directly as now, and Kafka only as an outward stream that other teams attach to.
